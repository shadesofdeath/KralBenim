### Narration (4485 chars, one flowing text)

August 1281. A bay off a small Japanese island called Takashima is packed with ships. Thousands, so close you could walk from deck to deck. And then the sky turns black. This was the biggest invasion fleet the world had ever seen. And in one night, most of it vanished. Japan called it kamikaze, the divine wind. But was Japan really saved by the gods? Divers have searched this seabed for forty years. And last month, they pulled up something nobody expected. It all started in 1268. Kublai Khan, grandson of Genghis Khan, ruled the largest empire on Earth. And he sent Japan a letter. It was polite. Then came the last line: nobody wants to use force, do they? Here's the thing. The real power in Japan was a samurai regent named Hojo Tokimune, and he was just seventeen. So what did he write back? Nothing, not a single word. More envoys came, and they went home empty-handed too. To a man who ruled half the world, silence was an answer. And it was the wrong one! In 1274, Kublai sent his ships. Historians think around 900 ships sailed from Korea, carrying maybe 30,000 men. In November, they landed at Hakata Bay. And the samurai were in for a shock. A samurai expected a battle to start with a challenge: one warrior, his name, his family. The invaders didn't care. They fought in tight blocks that moved to the beat of drums. They shot clouds of arrows. And they threw something Japan had never seen: clay bombs packed with gunpowder. We know because a samurai named Takezaki Suenaga paid for a scroll of his own deeds. And right above his wounded horse, a bomb is exploding. By nightfall, the defenders had fallen back. But the next morning, people looked out at the bay and froze. The fleet was gone. Some records say a storm caught it on the way home. Historians still argue about that storm. But one thing was sure: they would be back. So Japan did something simple. It built a wall. From 1276, workers stacked stone along the beaches of Hakata Bay, about twenty kilometers of it. Sheer on the sea side, sloped on the inside, so archers could run up and shoot. Every landowner in Kyushu had to guard his own stretch. And Kublai? He had just conquered southern China, which gave him something new: the shipyards of the Song dynasty. So this time, he didn't send one fleet. He sent two! In 1281, an eastern fleet of about 900 ships left Korea again. A far bigger fleet sailed from southern China. Old records claim 4,400 ships and over 100,000 men. Historians think the real numbers were smaller. The eastern fleet arrived first, in June. It sailed into Hakata Bay, and found the wall. There was nowhere to land! And at night, samurai rowed out in small boats, climbed aboard, fought, and vanished into the dark. So the fleet waited for weeks, tens of thousands of men on crowded decks in the summer heat. And that's what the divers found last month. In the stern of a third wreck: five tiny dice and 29 little game tiles. Some headlines called them playing cards. Soldiers, killing time. The southern fleet came late. In August, the two fleets gathered off Takashima, packed close together. But it was typhoon season. Around the fifteenth of August, the typhoon hit. Waves lifted ships and smashed them into each other and onto the rocks. Masts snapped. Hulls split open. When the storm passed, most of the fleet was gone. At least half the army was lost. Japan had survived, again. Temples had prayed for exactly this, so the storm got a holy name: kamikaze. But storms hit ships all the time. So why did this fleet break apart so completely? Since the 1980s, divers have searched the mud here. They've pulled up thousands of objects: helmets, swords, anchors, and the same clay bombs from the scroll. Then came real hulls, in 2011, 2014 and 2023. And the wood told a strange story. Many timbers came from small Yangtze River boats, some with flat bottoms. River boats, not ocean ships. Many seem to have lacked a strong keel, the spine that holds a sea ship together. Others were rushed out of the shipyards to meet a deadline. Kublai's fleet was huge on paper, but much of it was never built for the open sea, let alone a typhoon. So was Japan saved by a wind from the gods? By a storm, yes. But also by a stone wall that kept the fleet waiting for weeks, and by ships that were built to fail. What do you think really saved Japan: the storm, the wall, or the samurai? Tell me in the comments, and subscribe to Stickstory for more history that's stranger than the legend.
