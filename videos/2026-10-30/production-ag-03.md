## Narration (written first, then split; 4465 characters)

July 17th, 709 BC. In the state of Lu, in ancient China, the Sun went out in the middle of the day. The court scribes watched the light drain from the sky, and they wrote down one short line: the Sun was totally eclipsed. Here's the problem. Run that eclipse backwards on a computer, with Earth spinning at today's speed, and the shadow misses China completely. It lands thousands of kilometers away. So who got it wrong? The scribes, or the planet?

To answer that, you have to meet some accidental scientists. For those scribes, an eclipse was an omen, a warning from heaven, so it went straight into the court records. And they weren't alone. In Babylon, astronomers pressed eclipses into clay tablets for centuries, often with the time of day. Those tablets became one of the best clocks we have. Fast forward to 1695. The English astronomer Edmond Halley, yes, the comet guy, compared those old eclipses with his own tables. And something didn't add up. The Moon seemed to be speeding up over the centuries. Halley had found a real clue. But he was reading it backwards, because the Moon wasn't the only thing that had changed. The real culprit was right under his feet.

Here's the twist. Our planet is the clock, and that clock is running slow. And the thief? It's the Moon. Its gravity pulls the oceans into two huge bulges of water, the tides. Earth spins underneath them, and all that water dragging across the seafloor works like a brake. And that lost spin doesn't vanish. It pushes the Moon a little farther away every year. How do we know? In 1969, the Apollo 11 astronauts left a mirror on the Moon. Scientists still fire lasers at it and time the light bouncing back. The answer: the Moon moves away about 3.8 centimeters a year. That's about as fast as your fingernails grow. Now add that braking up. Each day gets longer by less than two thousandths of a second per century. Tiny, right? But those slivers pile up, day after day, for about a million days. By the time of the eclipse in Lu, the difference adds up to more than five and a half hours of spin. When a Japanese team led by Hisashi Hayakawa rechecked this record, they even had to move the old capital, Qufu, about eight kilometers on the map. Then the shadow fit perfectly. The scribes were right. But what if we could go back much, much further?

The proof was hiding in stone. In 1963, an American paleontologist named John Wells looked closely at fossil corals. Corals grow a bit like trees. They add a thin line every day, and a thicker band every year. So Wells counted the daily lines inside one yearly band. In living corals he found about 360, close to our 365. Then he counted corals from the Devonian, almost 400 million years ago. About 400 lines! The year itself takes about the same time as always. So the days had to be shorter, roughly 22 hours each. So the slowing is real. But it creates a very modern headache.

Because in 1955, the first caesium atomic clock started ticking in England. Suddenly we had a clock better than the planet. Atoms don't slow down. Earth does. So in 1972, timekeepers made a deal. Whenever Earth fell too far behind, they would add one extra second to the world's clocks. A leap second. The clock would tick fifty-eight, fifty-nine, sixty, and only then midnight. Sounds harmless, right? It's not. Computers hate a minute with sixty-one seconds. In 2012, one leap second helped crash Reddit and scrambled check-in systems at the airline Qantas. Since 1972, they added twenty-seven. The last one came on New Year's Eve, 2016. But then something strange happened.

Since 2020, Earth has actually been spinning a little faster. Some days in 2024 came out more than a thousandth of a second shorter than twenty-four hours. Timekeepers even started talking about the first negative leap second, maybe around 2029. And here's the weirdest part. Melting polar ice sends water toward the equator, and that slows Earth down again, like a figure skater opening her arms. So this October, the world's timekeepers met in Versailles and said: enough. No more leap seconds after May 2027. Clocks and planet can now drift apart by up to a whole hour. One day, someone may get a leap hour. And the Moon? It keeps braking, and your day keeps getting longer, just too slowly to notice. So, if you could add one extra hour to every day, what would you do with it? Tell me in the comments, and subscribe to Stickstory for more strange true stories about our planet.
