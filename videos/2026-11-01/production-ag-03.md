### Verified facts (with sources)
- Lighthouse first lit **7 Dec 1899**; tower 23 m, light **101 m (331 ft)** above sea; about **20 miles (32 km) west of Lewis**; automated **28 Sep 1971**. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flannan_Isles_Lighthouse
- **15 Dec 1900**: SS *Archtor* (Capt. Holman) passed and saw no light; reported after docking at Leith (18 Dec). Hesperus due 20 Dec, held at Breasclete by weather; arrived **26 Dec** (Boxing Day), noon; whistle + rocket/flare, no answer; no flag; no provision boxes at the landing. — Wikipedia; https://nrscotland.gov.uk/learning-and-events/research-guides/lighthouses/the-mystery-of-the-flannan-islands-lighthouse ("passed rock on 15 December and saw no light"; "running normally until the 15th"; Hesperus "11 days later")
- Relief keeper **Joseph Moore** landed first; gate and door closed; **kitchen clock stopped**; beds empty; "the fire was not lighted for some days"; pots and pans cleaned and put away; lamps cleaned and refilled; **MacArthur's coat left behind** ("he went out in shirt sleeves"); Ducat and Marshall gone in sea boots/oilskins. — https://userhome.brooklyn.cuny.edu/anthro/jbeatty/Scotia/issue38/issue38a.html (Moore's memo, 28 Dec 1900); Wikipedia; https://news.stv.tv/highlands-islands/mystery-of-eilean-mor-lighthouse-keepers-disappearance-as-records-released-for-first-time
- Keepers: **James Ducat** (principal), **Thomas Marshall** (assistant), **Donald MacArthur** (Occasional/stand-in). Ducat: wife + 4 children; MacArthur: wife + 2. — Wikipedia
- Captain Harvie's telegram: "A dreadful accident has happened at the Flannans." — Wikipedia
- Superintendent **Robert Muirhead** (had recruited all three) investigated; slate entries show the men on duty until dinner time on Sat **15 Dec**; report of **8 Jan 1901**: rope box in a crevice **110 ft** above sea smashed, contents strewn; iron railings bent; iron railway torn from its concrete; a rock of **over a ton** displaced; turf torn away **over 200 ft** up; damage "difficult to believe unless actually seen"; conclusion: "an extra large sea" swept them away. — Wikipedia; Scotia (Brooklyn) article ("unexpectedly large roller")
- Marshall had been fined **five shillings** for losing gear in a gale (James Love's research). — Wikipedia; https://www.historyhit.com/the-flannan-isle-mystery-when-three-lighthouse-keepers-vanished-overnight/
- **Myth vs record:** the uneaten meal and overturned chair come from **Wilfrid Gibson's 1912 ballad "Flannan Isle"**; the dramatic log entries ("God is over all") were shown by **Mike Dash (Fortean Times)** to be later fabrications. Sea serpent / spies / running away = rejected theories. No bodies were ever found. — Wikipedia; History Hit
- Not used (unverified): canary in a cage, "Phantom of the Seven Hunters", exact cliff heights other than the report's 110 ft / 200 ft.

### Full narration (one piece, read-aloud checked; 4485 characters)
December 26th, 1900. A relief ship rocked below a tiny island in the Atlantic, and nobody came down to meet it. The captain blew the ship's whistle. He fired a rocket into the grey sky. Nothing came back. No flag on the flagpole. No supply boxes waiting at the landing. So Joseph Moore jumped onto the rocks and climbed the steps to the lighthouse, alone. The gate was shut. The door was shut. And inside, the kitchen clock had stopped. Three keepers had vanished from a rock in the sea. Where did they go? Stay with me, because the truth is sadder than the legend. To see why this scared people so much, you have to see the place. The Flannan Isles sit about twenty miles west of the Isle of Lewis, off the northwest coast of Scotland. The biggest one, Eilean Mor, is a lump of rock walled in by cliffs. In December 1899, a brand-new lighthouse was lit on top, more than three hundred feet above the waves. Three men kept that light alive: James Ducat, Thomas Marshall, and Donald MacArthur, a stand-in keeper. Ducat had a wife and four children waiting at home. MacArthur had a wife and two. Their job: keep the lamp clean, keep it full of oil, and never let it go dark. But on the night of December 15th, the steamer Archtor passed the islands, and her crew saw something wrong. The light was out. The Archtor only reported it three days later, and a gale kept the relief ship, the Hesperus, stuck on Lewis. For eleven days, nobody knew. She finally reached the island at noon on Boxing Day. Let's go inside with Moore. The beds were empty, and the fire hadn't been lit for days. The kitchen was clean, the pots washed and put away, as if the men had finished dinner and stepped out. Up in the tower, the lamp was cleaned and filled, ready for a night that never came. And in the hallway, one coat was still hanging on its peg. Donald MacArthur's. So whoever went out last went out into a December storm in his shirtsleeves. Why would anyone do that? Moore and two sailors searched the whole island. No bodies, no message, nothing. The captain sent a telegram: a dreadful accident has happened at the Flannans. But what accident takes three men and leaves no trace? That's where the story started to grow. If you've heard this story before, you've probably heard a much creepier version. In that version, a meal sat uneaten and a chair lay knocked over, as if the men had fled in terror. Those details come from a poem: Flannan Isle, a ballad Wilfrid Gibson published in 1912. But Moore actually saw a clean kitchen, everything put away. Then came the logbook, with strange entries from the keepers' final days. A storm like nothing they had seen, Ducat quiet, MacArthur crying, prayers. And a last line: God is over all. It's chilling. It's also fake. The writer Mike Dash checked the records, and those entries were added much later. Then everyone piled on: a sea serpent, foreign spies, three men running off to new lives. But the man who actually walked that island never needed a monster. His name was Robert Muirhead. He had hired all three missing men himself. Their slate notes put the men on duty until dinner time on December 15th. The same day the light went out. Then he walked down to the west landing. A box of ropes, wedged in a crack a hundred and ten feet above the sea, was smashed open. Iron railings were bent, and part of the iron rail track was ripped out of its concrete. A rock weighing more than a ton had been moved. And at the top of the cliff, more than two hundred feet up, the turf was torn away. Muirhead admitted some of it was hard to believe unless you saw it. So what could reach that high? Here's what he worked out. Ducat and Marshall's oilskins and sea boots were gone. They went out dressed for the weather. Most likely, they went down to secure that box of ropes before the storm took it. And here's the detail that hurts. Marshall had once been fined five shillings for losing gear in a gale. Then, Muirhead believed, an extra large sea rushed up the face of the rock, and swept them away. And MacArthur? He may have seen the danger from above and run out, coatless, to warn them. No bodies were ever found. So here's the answer: they went down to the sea in a storm, and the sea came up to take them. The light still shines. It was automated in 1971, and nobody has lived on Eilean Mor since. So what do you think: one giant wave, or something the record missed? Tell me in the comments, and subscribe to Stickstory for more true mysteries.
