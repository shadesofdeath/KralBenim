### Narration (one flowing text, 4,487 chars incl. spaces; budget warning → low end)

Vienna, 1846. A young woman in labor stands at the gate of the biggest hospital in the city, and she is begging. Not for a doctor. She is begging, on her knees, not to be taken to the ward with the doctors. Because everyone in Vienna knew something the doctors didn't. In that ward, mothers died. So why was the clinic with the best-trained doctors the deadliest place to give birth? The answer was hiding in plain sight, but it would cost one man everything. The hospital had two maternity clinics, side by side. The First Clinic trained medical students. The Second Clinic trained midwives. And yet, in 1846, about one mother in nine who gave birth in the First Clinic died. In the Second Clinic, fewer than three in a hundred. The killer was childbed fever. It struck a few days after the birth, and then, very often, death. So women did the math. Some walked the streets for hours, so the baby would come on the way, and they would arrive afterwards. And here's the strange part. Those street births, with no help at all, almost never got the fever. So what did the First Clinic have that a cold street didn't? In July 1846, a young Hungarian doctor named Ignaz Semmelweis became an assistant in the First Clinic. And the deaths haunted him. He started treating the wards like a crime scene. Was it crowding? No. The Second Clinic was even more crowded. Then he noticed the priest. To reach the dying, the priest walked through the First Clinic ringing a little bell. Maybe the women were frightened to death? So Semmelweis asked him to take another route, with no bell. Nothing changed. In the Second Clinic, women gave birth lying on their side. He tried that too. Nothing. And then, in March 1847, the answer came from a death nobody expected. Jakob Kolletschka was a professor and a good friend of Semmelweis. During an autopsy, a student's scalpel slipped and nicked his finger. A tiny cut. Within days, Kolletschka was dead. Semmelweis read the autopsy report, and his blood ran cold. Inside, his friend's body looked just like the mothers'. Now think about that. Something from a dead body got in through a cut. And who touched dead bodies every morning? The doctors and students of the First Clinic. They did autopsies, then walked down the corridor and examined women in labor. The midwives never did autopsies. And the women on the street? Nobody examined them at all. Semmelweis called it cadaverous particles. He couldn't see them. But maybe he could wash them away. In May 1847, he put a bowl at the door of the ward. It held chlorinated lime, a kind of bleach. Every doctor and student had to scrub in it before touching a patient. It stung, and it smelled awful. And it worked. In April, almost one mother in five in the First Clinic had died. By June, about two in a hundred. In 1848, the whole year ended at just over one percent. You'd think Vienna would carry him through the streets. Instead, the doctors turned on him! Here's the problem. In 1847, nobody knew germs existed. Semmelweis could show that washing worked, but he couldn't explain why. And his idea carried a terrible message: the doctors were bringing death on their own hands. Many of them were offended. Doctors were gentlemen, and a gentleman's hands were not dirty. His boss, Professor Johann Klein, never accepted it. And for years, Semmelweis barely published a word. In 1849, Klein refused to renew his post. Semmelweis went home to Pest, in Hungary, and at Saint Rochus Hospital he did it again. Mothers stopped dying. In 1861 he finally wrote his book. Critics tore it apart, and he answered with furious open letters, calling them murderers. By 1865, his mind was failing. He was taken to an asylum in Vienna, and two weeks later he was dead, at only 47. The cause? An infected wound. And here's the cruel part. Within a few years, the world caught up. Louis Pasteur showed that tiny living germs cause disease. Joseph Lister began cleaning wounds with antiseptic. Germs explained everything Semmelweis had seen. Today he's called the savior of mothers. There's even a name for what happened to him: the Semmelweis reflex, rejecting new evidence because it clashes with old beliefs. So why was the best ward in Vienna the deadliest? Because of the doctors' own hands. And the cure was a bowl of bleach, plus the courage to admit you might be the problem. Would you have believed him in 1847? Tell me in the comments, and subscribe to Stickstory for more true stories that changed the world.

### Verified facts (sources)
- Semmelweis born 1 July 1818 (Buda), assistant at the First Obstetrical Clinic from 1 July 1846; died 13 August 1865, aged 47 (Wikipedia; MJA 2018).
- Yearly maternal mortality 1841–1846, First vs Second clinic: 7.8/3.5, 15.8/7.6, 9.0/6.0, 8.2/2.3, 6.9/2.0, 11.4/2.8 %; averages "about 10 %" vs "less than 4 %" (Wikipedia table from Semmelweis's 1861 book). 1846: 11.4 % ≈ 1 in 9; 2.8 % < 3 in 100.
- Women begged on their knees not to be admitted to the First Clinic; some gave birth in the street on purpose; street births rarely got the fever (Wikipedia; MJA: street-birth risk "significantly lower").
- Ruled out: overcrowding (Second Clinic more crowded), climate; priest walking through ringing a bell → asked to change route / stop the bell, no effect; lateral birth position as in the Second Clinic, no effect (GMS HIC / publisso, Ravel et al.; History.com for the bell).
- Kolletschka pricked by a student's scalpel during an autopsy, died 1847 (March 1847, commonly cited); his autopsy pathology resembled the mothers' (Wikipedia, Encyclopedia.com, History.com).
- Chlorinated lime handwashing from mid-May 1847; monthly First Clinic rates: April 1847 18.3 %, June 2.2 %, July 1.2 %, Aug 1.9 %; 1848 ≈ 1.27–1.3 % (Wikipedia; MJA "11.4 % in 1846 to 1.3 % in 1848").
- Some doctors offended; gentlemen's hands seen as clean (Wikipedia; History.com). Klein never accepted the theory, refused to reappoint him in March 1849; Semmelweis reluctant to publish (MJA, Encyclopedia.com). Left for Pest 1850; Szent Rókus from 1851 (Wikipedia, MJA). Book 1861, dismissed; angry open letters calling critics murderers (Wikipedia, MJA).
- 1865 committed to the asylum (Lazarettgasse, Vienna), died two weeks later of septicaemia from an infected wound (MJA; Wikipedia). Beating detail intentionally left out (respect).
- Pasteur / Lister / germ theory gave the explanation later (Wikipedia, MJA). "Semmelweis reflex" (Wikipedia).
- Dropped as unverified: "gentlemen's hands are clean" quote (Meigs), exact Kolletschka date on screen, 1847 yearly rate.
