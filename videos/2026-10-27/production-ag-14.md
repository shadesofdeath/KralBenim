## Verified facts (sources in the script)
- Six articles in The Sun (New York penny paper founded 1833 by Benjamin Day), Aug 25–31 1835; headline "Great Astronomical Discoveries Lately Made by Sir John Herschel"; presented as from the Edinburgh Journal of Science, which had ceased in 1833 (Museum of Hoaxes, Wikipedia).
- Day 1 telescope, lens 24 feet across, 7 tons (Smithsonian, Wikipedia). Day 2 forests, white sand beach, bison-like quadrupeds, single-horned goats "the bluish color of lead" (Smithsonian). Day 3 biped beaver carrying its young "like a human being"; huts with smoke, "acquainted with the use of fire" (Smithsonian, Alpha History quote). Day 4 (Fri Aug 28) Vespertilio-homo, "doubtless innocent and happy creatures" (Smithsonian). Day 5 sapphire temple, Day 6 (Mon Aug 31) narrative ends after an accident with the lens burned the observatory (Museum of Hoaxes; Wikipedia).
- The Sun (Aug 28) claimed the greatest circulation of any daily in the world; also sold the series as a pamphlet (Museum of Hoaxes). Exact circulation jump deliberately NOT stated (historians say it was exaggerated).
- Rivals reprinted it; some treated it as true (Museum of Hoaxes list). Sept 16 1835: The Sun said correspondents urged it to confess a hoax and refused until British papers confirmed it; never retracted (Museum of Hoaxes; Wikipedia).
- Richard Adams Locke, Cambridge-educated Sun reporter, almost certainly the author; later said it was satire against lunar-life speculation (Thomas Dick) (History.com, Museum of Hoaxes, Smithsonian).
- Herschel went to the Cape in (January) 1834; son of William Herschel (Uranus). Laughed when shown it, feared his real results would look humble; 1837 letter to his aunt: "pestered from all quarters with that ridiculous hoax about the Moon — in English French Italian & German" (Museum of Hoaxes, Clements Library).
- Gruithuisen claimed a lunar "city" (Wallwerk) in 1824; Thomas Dick computed 21,894,974,404,480 inhabitants of the solar system (Wikipedia) → "almost twenty-two trillion".
- Unsure / avoided: "six cents" for the older papers is the standard textbook figure (sixpenny papers); the Yale-professors anecdote is doubtful and left out; no circulation numbers.

## Narration (written first, as one text; round 2: 4996 characters)

In 1835, a New York newspaper told its readers there were people on the Moon. With wings. Today, SpaceX and Blue Origin are getting ready to build a real base up there. But back then, thousands of New Yorkers believed the Moon was already taken. Picture it. You pay one cent for a newspaper, and you read that a famous astronomer has just seen them. Creatures about four feet tall, with faces almost like ours and wings like a bat. The paper even gave them a scientific name: Vespertilio-homo, the man-bat. And here's the crazy part. People believed it. So how does a story this wild end up on breakfast tables across America? And why did the paper that printed it never take it back? To answer that, you have to see New York in 1835. The city was loud, crowded and hungry for news. Most newspapers cost six cents, which was real money back then, and they were full of shipping lists and politics. Then, in 1833, a young printer named Benjamin Day started a paper called The Sun, and sold it for a single penny. Newsboys shouted it on every corner. It was cheap, it was fun, and it loved a good story. But a penny paper lives or dies by one thing: how many copies it sells tomorrow. Meanwhile, on the other side of the planet, one of the most famous scientists alive had gone out of reach. Sir John Herschel, son of the man who discovered Uranus, had sailed to the Cape of Good Hope with a big telescope to map the southern sky. And I want you to remember this: Herschel was real, he was respected, and he was very far away. A letter from the Cape took many weeks to reach New York. Nobody could just ask him. And someone at The Sun counted on it! On Tuesday, August 25th, 1835, The Sun ran a long story under a very serious headline. Great Astronomical Discoveries Lately Made by Sir John Herschel. It claimed to be reprinted from the respected Edinburgh Journal of Science. The first day was all about the machine: a telescope lens twenty-four feet across, weighing seven tons. No life yet, just numbers and serious language. Smart, right? The real show was still coming. On day two, the lens turned to the Moon and found forests, a white sand beach, and herds of brown animals that looked like bison. Then goats with a single horn, the bluish color of lead. Day three got stranger: a beaver that walked on two legs and carried its young in its arms like a human being. Their huts even had smoke rising from them. These beavers knew fire! Then came Friday, August 28th, and the man-bats. Winged people who walked upright and, in the paper's own words, were “doubtless innocent and happy creatures.” Day five brought a temple made of polished sapphire. Who built it? The story never really said. And on the last day, an accident with the giant lens set the observatory on fire, and the reports stopped. How convenient. By then, The Sun was bragging that it outsold every daily paper in the world, and it reprinted the series as a pamphlet. Rival papers copied it, and some treated it as real news. But a few editors smelled something wrong. So why did so many people fall for it? It's easy to laugh today, but honestly, I think I would have believed it too. Look at what readers had in front of them. First, the name. Herschel was real, famous, and really was at the Cape with a big telescope. And he couldn't answer. Second, the source. The Edinburgh Journal of Science sounded perfect. Here's the twist: it had stopped publishing in 1833. Third, the details. Lens sizes, weights, the color of the goats. And too much detail feels like truth. Fourth, the part people forget: in 1824, a German astronomer, Franz von Gruithuisen, claimed he had spotted a city on the Moon. And a popular Scottish writer, Thomas Dick, had calculated the solar system held almost twenty-two trillion inhabitants. So the man-bats were not a crazy new idea. They were the answer people were already waiting for. But who actually wrote them? And did The Sun ever admit the truth? Let's start with the writer. It was almost certainly Richard Adams Locke, a Cambridge-educated reporter at The Sun. He later called it satire, aimed at serious books about life on other worlds. The joke simply worked too well. On September 16th, The Sun answered its critics. Some readers, it said, wanted it to confess the whole thing was a hoax. It refused, and told them to wait for the British papers. It never printed a retraction. And Herschel? At the Cape, a visitor handed him the story. He laughed, and said his real discoveries would look humble next to it. But two years later he complained to his aunt that he was pestered from all quarters with that ridiculous hoax about the Moon, in four languages. Today real rockets are being built for the Moon, and the old recipe still works: a famous name, a faraway place, and lots of confident detail. So, if you had bought The Sun in August 1835, would you have believed in the man-bats? Tell me in the comments, and subscribe to Stickstory for more true stories that sound made up.
