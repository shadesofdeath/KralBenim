## Narration (written first, as one text; round 2 version, 5000 characters)

In 1951, two physicists at Los Alamos asked permission to set off an atomic bomb, and drop a detector down a hole beside it. And their boss said yes! Why? Because they were hunting the shyest thing in the universe. Here's the strange part. Right now, about a hundred trillion of them fly through your body every second, and you'll never feel one. The New York Times recently asked it in one headline. Neutrinos are everywhere. Now what? To answer it, I'll take you into a thirty-year mystery: two out of three neutrinos from the Sun seemed to vanish. Where did they go? It started with a crisis, in 1930. Radioactive atoms spat out electrons, and the energy didn't add up. Some of it went missing, every time. Even Niels Bohr was ready to give up a sacred rule: energy never disappears. Then Wolfgang Pauli had an idea. He skipped a physics meeting for a ball in Zurich and sent a letter instead. It opened with five famous words: Dear Radioactive Ladies and Gentlemen. He imagined a tiny particle with no charge and almost no mass, slipping out unseen with the missing energy. He called it a desperate remedy. The story goes that he felt guilty. I have done a terrible thing, he told a friend. I have invented a particle that cannot be detected. Enrico Fermi later named it the neutrino, the little neutral one. But for decades, nobody could catch one. Back to Los Alamos, and to Frederick Reines and Clyde Cowan. The logic: if neutrinos almost never hit anything, you need a flood of them, and nothing floods like a nuclear explosion. The plan put a twenty-kiloton bomb on a tower, with a deep shaft about forty meters away. At the blast, the detector would fall freely down the shaft, so the shock wave couldn't shake it. They started digging. Then a colleague suggested a reactor instead: fewer neutrinos, but a steady stream, day and night. Here's the part I love. A neutrino hitting a proton in water makes two flashes of light, a few millionths of a second apart. One flash could be anything. Two flashes in that rhythm? That's a fingerprint. In 1956, they set up beside a giant reactor at Savannah River, South Carolina. They put tanks of water and cadmium between tanks of glowing liquid, watched by a hundred and ten light sensors. And it worked: about three neutrinos an hour. Can you imagine sitting up all night for that? On June 14, 1956, they telegraphed Pauli. We have definitely detected neutrinos. His reply? Everything comes to him who knows how to wait. The ghost was real. But the biggest neutrino factory of all is right above your head. Deep in the Sun's core, hydrogen fuses into helium, and every reaction throws out neutrinos. About sixty-five billion of them cross every square centimeter of you each second. That's roughly your fingernail. In the 1960s, a chemist named Ray Davis decided to count them. His detector was a little absurd. A tank longer than a city bus, holding a hundred thousand gallons of dry-cleaning fluid, nearly a mile down the Homestake gold mine. Why so deep? The rock stops almost everything that rains down from space. Neutrinos go right through. That fluid is full of chlorine. Once in a long while, a neutrino turns one chlorine atom into argon. Every few weeks, Davis bubbled helium through the tank to fish them out. A handful of atoms in six hundred tons of liquid! The theorist John Bahcall calculated what he should find. But in 1968, Davis found only a third. So who was wrong? Many blamed the experiment, others blamed the Sun. And for decades, nobody could close the gap. The answer came from two more giant tanks, deep in the dark. In Japan, Super-Kamiokande held fifty thousand tons of water under eleven thousand light sensors. In 1998, Takaaki Kajita's team saw neutrinos made in our sky vanish too, mostly ones crossing the whole Earth. The longer the trip, the more went missing. Then in Canada, two kilometers down a nickel mine, SNO could count every kind of neutrino. By 2001, the answer was in. The missing neutrinos had never been missing. Neutrinos come in three types, called flavors. On the trip from the Sun, they keep switching, and Davis's tank could only see one. So Davis was right. Bahcall was right. The neutrino was playing tricks. And to switch like that, a neutrino must have mass. Tiny, but not the zero our best theory predicted. Davis shared the 2002 Nobel Prize with Japan's Masatoshi Koshiba, at eighty-seven. Kajita and Arthur McDonald won in 2015. But the ghost isn't done with us. So, neutrinos are everywhere. Now what? We still don't know what a neutrino weighs, or whether it explains why the universe is made of matter. A new experiment, DUNE, will fire neutrinos thirteen hundred kilometers through the Earth, from near Chicago into the old Homestake mine. The mine where Ray Davis counted argon atoms one by one. This time, the detector is made of argon. So, would you have approved the bomb, or the reactor? Tell me in the comments, and subscribe to Stickstory for a true story like this every day.

## Facts checked

- Pauli's letter of 4 Dec 1930 opens "Dear Radioactive Ladies and Gentlemen"; he could not attend the Tübingen meeting because
  of a ball in Zurich (6/7 Dec); calls the idea a "desperate remedy" (RHUL transcription; CERN timeline).
- "I have done a terrible thing… a particle that cannot be detected": widely quoted, told via Walter Baade, source not documented
  → narrated as "The story goes that…".
- Niels Bohr was ready to give up strict energy conservation in beta decay (standard history; told without a quote).
- Fermi named it "neutrino" (Italian, "little neutral one"), 1933–34. "For twenty-five years nobody could catch one" (1930 → 1956 = 26; kept vague).
- 1951 bomb plan: 20-kiloton device on a tower, detector dropped in free fall down an evacuated shaft about 40 m away
  (sources say 40 m or ~50 m → "about forty meters"); approved by lab director Norris Bradbury; digging began; J. M. B. Kellogg urged a reactor.
- Savannah River 1956: two water tanks (~200 l) with ~40 kg cadmium chloride between three liquid-scintillator tanks,
  110 five-inch photomultipliers; about 3 events per hour (HyperPhysics). Telegram 14 June 1956 ("…we have definitely detected
  neutrinos…"); reply "Everything comes to him who knows how to wait" (CERN Courier; in2p3 history).
- 100 trillion neutrinos through a body per second (Live Science; Chodos & Riordon). ~65 billion solar neutrinos per cm² per second
  (pp flux alone 6.1×10¹⁰; Wikipedia "Solar neutrino") → "about sixty-five billion".
- Homestake: ~100,000 gallons (380 m³, ~615 t) of perchloroethylene, 4,850 ft (1,478 m) down; tank 6.1 m × 14.6 m (longer than a
  12 m city bus); helium purges every few weeks; ~1/3 of Bahcall's prediction; first result 1968 (Wikipedia; Brookhaven; Symmetry).
- Super-Kamiokande: 50,000 t ultra-pure water, ~1 km down, 11,146 PMTs ("eleven thousand"); 1998 atmospheric-neutrino
  oscillation, deficit of upward-going neutrinos (Kajita). SNO: 1,000 t heavy water, ~2 km down in the Creighton nickel mine,
  counts all flavors; 2001 result. Nobel 2015: Kajita and McDonald (Physics World). Davis Nobel 2002, born Oct 1914 → 87.
- Oscillation implies mass; the Standard Model had massless neutrinos. Solar neutrinos travel ~8 min (not narrated as a number).
- DUNE: beam from Fermilab (near Chicago) ~1,300 km to SURF in the former Homestake mine; far detector uses liquid argon.
- Myths avoided: no "light-year of lead" figure; the bomb was never used ("asked permission", "the plan").
