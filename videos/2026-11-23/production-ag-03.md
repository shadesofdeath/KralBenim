### Narration (one flowing text, 4,999 chars)

Near midnight on the twenty-ninth of November, 1944, snow was falling on a quiet bay in Maine. And out in the black water, a German submarine rose to the surface.
A rubber boat pushed off from it. Inside sat two men dressed for a city street, not a snowstorm, each holding a heavy suitcase.
What was in them? Sixty thousand American dollars, and a little packet of diamonds.
These two were Hitler's spies, and they had come to stay for two years.
They lasted about a month. So how does a mission this expensive fall apart that fast, and who gave them away? Stick with me.
Picture Hancock Point in 1944: a tiny summer village, almost empty in winter.
The war felt far away. But it wasn't. German U-boats hunted ships off this coast, and the one in the bay that night was U-1230.
It had crossed the Atlantic for almost two months with one secret job: deliver two passengers.
Germany had tried this once, in 1942, and the FBI caught all eight saboteurs. So why try again? Because this time, one spy was American.
William Colepaugh was born in Connecticut, studied at MIT, and served in the U.S. Navy. But he admired Nazi Germany, and in 1944 he switched sides.
His partner, Erich Gimpel, was a German radio expert and an experienced spy.
The plan? Live in New York, collect secrets about shipyards, airplane factories and rockets, and send them home by radio and invisible ink.
On paper, it looked perfect. But Germany made one huge mistake. They trusted the American.
Back to that snowy night. The two men climbed off the rocks onto a dark country road.
Headlights came around the bend. Mary Forni, driving home, saw two strangers in city clothes walking through the snow at midnight. She didn't stop, but she remembered.
Then a 17-year-old Boy Scout, Harvard Hodgkins, passed the same two men. City coats and big suitcases, in a snowstorm, in an empty winter village? That made no sense.
So he did something brilliant. He turned around and followed their footprints all the way back to the water.
His father was the deputy sheriff. So the local kid cracks the case, right? Not quite. Days passed before anyone acted. The spies were long gone.
They had walked five miles to the main road, taken a taxi to Bangor, then trains to Boston and New York.
And in New York, the real trouble began.
They rented a Manhattan apartment under fake names. Gimpel bought radio parts for a transmitter that could reach Germany.
Colepaugh had other plans. Nightclubs, bars, shows, new clothes. Within weeks, thousands of dollars of Hitler's money were gone.
Then, on the twenty-first of December, Colepaugh picked up the suitcases and simply walked out.
And he was about to do the one thing a spy should never do.
Two days before Christmas, he knocked on the door of an old school friend in Queens, and he told him everything. The submarine, the money, the mission.
His friend didn't believe him. Who would? But Colepaugh told it again, sober this time, and his friend said, you have to go to the FBI.
On the evening of December twenty-sixth, FBI agents took Colepaugh into custody.
Here's the answer to our big question. The mission didn't fall apart because of the snow, the Boy Scout, or brilliant detective work. It fell apart because one of the spies gave himself up.
And then he helped the FBI catch his partner.
He told the agents about one tiny habit. Gimpel read Spanish, and he liked to buy Peruvian newspapers at a newsstand in Times Square.
So the agents watched that newsstand, and waited.
On the thirtieth of December, a man stepped up for his Peruvian paper. As he reached into his coat for money, the agents grabbed him. It was Gimpel.
One month and one day after the submarine surfaced, both spies were locked up. But now they faced something much worse than prison.
In February 1945, a military court in New York found them guilty, and both men were sentenced to hang in April.
Then, on the twelfth of April, President Franklin Roosevelt died, just days before the executions. Everything was put on hold.
Within weeks, Germany surrendered, and President Harry Truman changed both sentences to life in prison. Gimpel was freed in 1955, Colepaugh in 1960.
But U-1230 had left something else behind in Maine. Something that stayed hidden for over eighty years.
Four days after the landing, on the third of December, the same submarine torpedoed a Canadian cargo ship, the SS Cornwallis. Of the 48 people on board, only five survived.
For decades, nobody knew exactly where she lay. In 2019, a team of civilian divers, the Nomad Exploration Team, began to search. It took seven years of sonar scans.
This July, they finally found her, almost four hundred feet down in the Gulf of Maine.
A submarine, two months at sea, sixty thousand dollars. And it all came undone because one spy decided he had made a huge mistake.
If you had been Harvard Hodgkins that night, would you have followed those footprints? Tell me in the comments, and subscribe to Stickstory for more of history's strangest true stories.
