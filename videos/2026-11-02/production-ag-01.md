## Facts (verified by the writer)
- 15 August 1977, Ohio State University's Big Ear radio telescope; the burst lasted 72 seconds and appeared on one of its 50 channels;
  the printout code "6EQUJ5"; astronomer Jerry Ehman circled it and wrote "Wow!" in red in the margin "a few days later"; never observed
  again despite repeated attempts (History.com: https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/august-15/wow-signal-detected).
- 72 s = the time Earth's rotation carried the fixed telescope's beam across the source (rose 36 s, fell 36 s); the characters encode
  intensity (digits < 10, letters ≥ 10), not a message; the source lay in Sagittarius; later searches (Robert Gray and others) found
  nothing (ExplorersWeb: https://explorersweb.com/exploration-mysteries-wow-signal/).
- 2024: Méndez, Ortiz Ceballos, Zuluaga, "Arecibo Wow! I" (arXiv 2408.08513, https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2408.08513) — Arecibo data from
  2020 show similar but much weaker narrowband signals from cold hydrogen clouds; they *hypothesize* a sudden brightening of such a cloud,
  triggered for example by a magnetar flare. Preprint, hypothesis only (Universe Today:
  https://www.universetoday.com/168176/the-wow-signal-deciphered-it-was-hydrogen-all-along/). Script says "a study suggested … Maybe."
- Not used: "volunteer" (no source confirms Ehman's status), the exact number of days ("Days later" dropped for budget; History.com says a
  few days), the alien claim, the 1420 MHz figure (true, but not needed).
- Big Ear was demolished in 1998: the "still listening" scenes show a generic modern dish, not Big Ear.
