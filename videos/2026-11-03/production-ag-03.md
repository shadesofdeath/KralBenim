### Narration (one flowing text, 4374 chars, 45 scenes, ~5:24 by preview estimate)

Picture a room so dark you can't see your own hand. Silk threads hang from the ceiling, dozens of them. And through that room, a bat flies, again and again, and never touches a single thread. It's 1793 in Pavia, Italy, and the man watching is a priest named Lazzaro Spallanzani. He can't see the threads. The bat can't see them either. So how is it doing that? It took science 145 years to answer that. And the answer was in the room the whole time. Nobody could hear it. To see why this drove him crazy, we have to start with an owl. Spallanzani was a priest, but he was also one of the most restless scientists in Europe. One night he let an owl fly in a room and made it completely dark. No candle, no moonlight. The great night hunter was helpless. It flew straight into the walls. So he tried the same thing with bats, and the bats flew perfectly. Now, this next part is hard to hear. To rule out the eyes, he took away the bats' sight. It was cruel, even then. But the result stunned him. They still dodged every thread. He even coated bats in varnish and flour paste, to block their sense of touch. They still flew. So he wrote down a strange idea: maybe bats have a sense that we simply don't have. But one letter was about to change that. It reached a doctor in Geneva named Louis Jurine, and Jurine asked a simple question. What about the ears? He gently plugged the bats' ears with wax. And this time, the bats were lost. They bumped into walls, into furniture, into everything. Jurine wrote that hearing seemed to take the place of sight. Spallanzani repeated the tests and agreed. So bats see with their ears. But that made no sense at all, because as far as anyone could tell, bats were silent. If a bat makes no sound, what could its ears possibly be listening to? But that was exactly the weak spot the most powerful scientist in France was waiting for. Georges Cuvier was the giant of natural history in Paris. And he did not buy it. In 1795 he offered a simpler answer: touch. The wings, he said, feel tiny changes in the air. Spallanzani had already tested touch, but it didn't matter. Cuvier was famous, and hearing was ridiculed. The touch theory won, and it ruled for more than a hundred years. Then in 1912, the same year the Titanic sank, the inventor Hiram Maxim had a wild thought. What if bats send out sound and feel the echo? And what if ships did the same, to find icebergs in the dark? He was so close. But he guessed the sound was too low for us to hear, made by beating wings. In 1920, a British scientist, Hamilton Hartridge, flipped it. What if the sound is too high for us to hear? He was right. But nobody could prove it, because nobody owned a machine that could hear that high. Until a student walked into a lab at Harvard. It's 1938. Donald Griffin is a Harvard student, and he is obsessed with bats. Down the hall, the physicist George Washington Pierce had built something rare: a detector for sounds above human hearing. Griffin carried a cage of bats into Pierce's lab and held one up to the machine. And the speaker came alive. Click, click, click. Bursts of sound no human ear had ever heard. For 145 years, bats had been shouting in every cave and every summer night. We were simply deaf to it. A few years later, Griffin and his friend Robert Galambos ran the final test. With ears blocked or mouths covered, the bats crashed. So bats shout, and listen for the echo. By 1944 Griffin had a name for it: echolocation. Even then, many scientists hated the idea. Griffin said it seemed not just unlikely, but emotionally repugnant. So how good is this hidden sonar, really? Here's the crazy part. Those silent bats are some of the loudest animals on Earth. Scientists in Panama measured calls above 140 decibels, right at the mouth. That's louder than a rock concert. And from those echoes, a bat can find a tiny moth in total darkness, tell how far away it is, and which way it's moving. Dolphins do it underwater. Ships use sonar. And some blind people click their tongues and listen to the echoes, just like a bat. So think back to that dark room in Pavia. The answer was flying past Spallanzani's ear the whole time. He just couldn't hear it. So what other sounds do you think are all around us right now, that we simply can't hear? Tell me in the comments, and subscribe to Stickstory for more of nature's hidden superpowers.

### Facts (verified)

- 1793, Pavia: Lazzaro Spallanzani (ordained priest, professor at Pavia) tests bats one sense at a time, wrote to fellow
  scientists in autumn 1793; blinded bats still avoided obstacles incl. silk threads; varnish + flour paste did not stop
  flight; "some new organ or sense which we do not have". Owls disoriented in complete darkness (no moon/starlight).
  https://antonspice.substack.com/p/the-gruesome-prehistory-of-bat-echolocation ·
  https://www.biosonar.bris.ac.uk/chapters/2/spallanzani1.htm · https://www.italyonthisday.com/2017/02/lazzaro-spallanzani-priest-and-scientist.html?m=1
- Louis Jurine (Geneva) plugged bats' ears (wax, turpentine, pomatum) → collisions; 1798 publication: "the organ of
  hearing appears to supply that of sight". Spallanzani + Jurine concluded hearing. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_echolocation ·
  https://www.biosonar.bris.ac.uk/chapters/2/spallanzani5.htm · https://www.aaas.org/discovering-sonar-bats
- Cuvier: 1795 paper, touch hypothesis (wings sense the air); ridiculed Spallanzani; view prevailed 100+ years
  (AAAS: "for the next 140 years ... touch"). biosonar spallanzani5 · substack above.
- Hiram Maxim 1912 (Scientific American, 27 July 1912, "Preventing Collisions at Sea: A Mechanical Application of the
  Bat's Sixth Sense"; Titanic sank April 1912): echo idea, but low (5–20 Hz) sound from wingbeats, felt by touch.
  https://www.scientificamerican.com/author/hiram-maxim/ · https://www.biosonar.bris.ac.uk/chapters/2/maxim3.htm
- Hamilton Hartridge 1920 proposed sound above human hearing. Wikipedia Animal echolocation.
- 1938: Harvard undergraduate Donald Griffin brought bats to physicist G. W. Pierce's ultrasound detector; the "silent"
  bats were emitting intense ultrasound. Later with Robert Galambos: deafened or gagged bats collided. Griffin coined
  "echolocation" (by 1944; Science 1944 "Echolocation by blind men, bats, and radar"). Quote: "not only implausible but
  emotionally repugnant". https://www.aaas.org/discovering-sonar-bats · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Griffin ·
  https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/11429/chapter/11
- Loudness: >140 dB SPL at 10 cm, 11 insect-eating bat species in Panama (Surlykke & Kalko, PLoS ONE 2008); rock concert
  115–120 dB. https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2008/04/080429204244.htm
- Echoes give distance, size, texture, velocity (AAAS). Some blind people echolocate with clicks (Wikipedia).
- Dropped as unverified: Daniel Kish by name, the "seized Galambos by the shoulders" anecdote, exact owl candle detail,
  "the room filled with clicks" (1938 detector only caught bats held close, not in flight → script says "held one up").
