### Narration (4440 chars, one flowing text; lint/preview count 4,433)

This brass padlock spent more than four hundred years at the bottom of the sea. It came from the Ann Francis, a ship that wrecked off South Wales in 1583. And it was still locked. No keyhole, just rings of letters. So how do you open a lock when whoever knew the code died centuries ago? Scientists at Cardiff University found out without even touching it. A micro CT scanner built a 3D picture of the inside. The notches lined up, and the code appeared: A, N, C, E. Here's the thing. Every lock ever made has a weakness. And for sixty-one years, one lock in a London shop window promised a fortune to anyone who could find it. Nobody could, until a man arrived from America. Who was he, and how did he do it? To answer that, we need to go way back.
Picture a door in the ancient world, with everything you own behind it. How do you keep it safe? One of the oldest locks ever found comes from the ruins of a palace at Khorsabad, in what is now Iraq. And it was made of wood. A wooden bolt slid across the door. When it closed, small wooden pins dropped into holes in the bolt and jammed it. To open it, you pushed in a big wooden key with pegs on top. The pegs lifted the pins, and the bolt slid free. People call it the Egyptian lock, because it was so common in Egypt. Remember those pins. But wood is clumsy. So what did the Romans do?
They made locks out of metal. And they added wards: little ridges inside the lock that block any key without the right slots. Some Roman keys were so small that people wore them on their fingers as rings. For centuries, locksmiths made beautiful iron locks with more and more wards. But here's the problem. Wards only block the key. They don't guard the bolt. A thief just needed a bent piece of metal that slipped around them. So a lock looked strong, but it was easy to beat. Then a London engineer decided to fix that for good.
His name was Joseph Bramah, and he patented his lock in 1784. It used a tiny tube-shaped key. Inside, sliders all had to be pushed to exactly the right depth at once. One slider off by a hair, and nothing turned. In 1790, Bramah was so sure of it that he put a padlock in his Piccadilly shop window, with a sign. It said: The artist who can make an instrument that will pick or open this lock shall receive 200 guineas the moment it is produced. That was a fortune. Bramah died in 1814, and the lock was still waiting. In 1818, after thieves used false keys at Portsmouth Dockyard, Jeremiah Chubb won a government prize with his detector lock. Lift a lever too high, and it jammed and gave the thief away. So by 1851, London had two unpickable locks. But that summer, the whole world came to town.
The Great Exhibition opened in a giant hall of glass and iron: the Crystal Palace. Among the visitors was a locksmith from Boston named Alfred Charles Hobbs. He came to sell American locks. Instead, he picked one. In front of astonished onlookers, Hobbs opened a Chubb detector lock in about 25 minutes. When they asked him to do it again, he did it in seven. But the big prize was still in that shop window, so Hobbs took the challenge. He could use any tools, but the lock had to survive unharmed. He worked for about 51 hours, spread over 16 days. And then the lock opened. After sixty-one years, the unpickable lock was picked. Bramah's side was furious: no real burglar gets sixteen days alone with a lock. Fair point. But the lesson stuck: every lock can be picked. It's only a question of time. So what do you put on your door after that?
The answer came from America, and it was very old. In 1848, a locksmith named Linus Yale patented a pin tumbler lock, built on the Egyptian idea: pins that drop and jam, and a key that lifts them. In the 1860s, his son, Linus Yale Junior, made it small. He put the pins in a little cylinder and shrank the big wooden key into flat metal with a jagged edge. Each bump lifts one pin to exactly the right height, and the cylinder turns. Go look at your front door key. Those bumps are pin heights. You carry a four thousand year old idea in your pocket. And yes, it can be picked too. Which brings us back to Wales. The Ann Francis belonged to Francis Shaxton, a merchant said to be a smuggler, and the padlock probably guarded a chest. And nobody knows why its owner chose those four letters. So what do you think A, N, C, E meant? Tell me in the comments, and subscribe to Stickstory for more everyday things with wild stories.
