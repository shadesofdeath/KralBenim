### Narration (one flowing text)

In January 1976, a Canadian scientist sat in a forest high in Mexico's mountains, and every tree around him was orange. Not leaves. Butterflies. Millions of them, hanging in heavy clusters. Then a branch above him snapped under their weight, and thousands of them tumbled down at his feet. He picked one up and froze. On its wing was a tiny white tag. His tag. So how does an insect lighter than a paperclip find this one forest, when it has never been here before? Nobody has fully answered that yet. This autumn, scientists put tiny solar radio tags on nearly 1,750 monarchs, just to follow them south. To see why that took forty years to answer, let's start in a summer meadow. A summer monarch lives only a few weeks. It hatches, eats, turns into a butterfly, lays eggs, and dies. Three or four generations come and go like that. Its caterpillars eat only milkweed, which is poisonous, so birds learn to leave monarchs alone. Then, at the end of summer, a different generation hatches. It lives up to eight months, and it flies south. Some fly up to 3,000 miles, from Canada to the mountains of central Mexico. And here's the crazy part. None of them has made this trip before. The last ones to fly it were their great-great-grandparents. So where were they all going? For most of the twentieth century, no scientist could tell you. But in Toronto, one young man refused to let that question go. Fred Urquhart, a Canadian zoologist, started chasing the monarchs in 1937. His wife Norah later joined the hunt. But how do you follow a butterfly? They used tiny sticky labels folded over the edge of a wing, each with a number and a Toronto address. Then they asked for help. Hundreds of volunteers caught monarchs, tagged them, and let them go. Whenever someone found a tag, they mailed it back. For years, the letters piled up in Toronto. Bit by bit, the returns drew a line on the map: southwest, through Texas, toward Mexico. And then the line just stopped. Thousands of tagged monarchs flew into Mexico, and not one turned up there. So where did they go? So in 1972, the Urquharts tried something new. They placed a notice in a Mexico City newspaper, asking for help to find the butterflies. An American engineer named Ken Brugger read it. He lived in Mexico, and he loved nature. In 1973, he talked Catalina Aguado into joining him. They married and kept searching together. And here's the twist. The families living in those mountains had known about the butterflies for generations. Only the scientists didn't. On the second of January 1975, Ken and Catalina climbed a mountain in Michoacán, and suddenly every tree was covered in butterflies. By one estimate, about sixty million monarchs were resting there. The message that reached Toronto was short: We have found them! Millions of monarchs, in evergreens beside a mountain clearing. But Fred still needed proof. Were these really the same butterflies from Canada and the United States? Only one of his tags could tell him. A year later, Fred and Norah went to see it themselves. And that brings us back to that snapping branch. That butterfly had been tagged the September before in Chaska, Minnesota, by a teacher and two students, about 1,750 miles away. It was the first tagged monarch ever found in Mexico. Fred had his proof. But it opened an even bigger puzzle. How does a butterfly that has never been here find the way? The first part of the answer is the sun. Monarchs use it as a compass. But the sun moves, from east to west. Follow it blindly, and you'd fly in a big curve. So to use the sun, you need a clock, to know what time it is and how much to correct. In 2009, Steven Reppert's team found where the monarch keeps that clock. Not in its brain. In its antennae. When they painted some monarchs' antennae black, so no light got in, those butterflies lost their way. In 2014, the same lab found a backup: a magnetic compass, also in the antennae, so even under thick clouds, a monarch keeps heading south. A sun compass, a clock and a magnetic compass. But a compass only tells you which way to go. It doesn't tell you where to stop. And the place where they stop is getting smaller. Every winter, the eastern monarchs gather on a few mountains west of Mexico City. In 2008, those forests became a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Scientists measure the colonies by the forest they cover: about 45 acres in the mid-1990s, and just over seven last winter, even after a 64 percent jump. Since the mid-1990s, the eastern monarchs have dropped by more than 80 percent, hit by lost milkweed, pesticides, logging and wild weather. That's why those radio tags matter: about 175 have already pinged receivers in Mexico. So how does a monarch find that one mountain? A clock in its antennae, the sun and a magnetic backup get it close, but those last few miles? Honestly, nobody fully knows. Have you ever seen a monarch where you live? Tell me in the comments, and subscribe to Stickstory for more of nature's strange superpowers.
