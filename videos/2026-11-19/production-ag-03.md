> It's the middle of the night on a frozen mountain, and it's about thirty below zero. Inside a small tent, someone grabs a knife and slices the wall open. From the inside. Nine young hikers crawl out into the dark. No boots, no coats. Some are only in socks. And they walk away from the only shelter for miles. None of them will come back. So what makes nine experienced hikers cut their way out of their own tent? Science may finally have an answer. And not everyone believes it. Let's go back to January 1959, in the Soviet Union. Ten students and graduates from the Ural Polytechnic Institute set off on skis into the northern Urals. Their leader was Igor Dyatlov, twenty-three years old, and a seriously good skier. Their goal was Mount Otorten, on a route rated Category Three, the toughest grade back then. These weren't beginners. They had done hard winter trips before. But on the twenty-eighth of January, Yuri Yudin got sick and had to turn back. He said goodbye and skied away. He had no idea that this goodbye would save his life. Four days later, the other nine reached a mountain the Mansi people called Kholat Syakhl, often translated as Dead Mountain. They would never leave it. On the first of February, they pitched their tent on its open slope. To make a flat spot, they dug a cut into the snow. Remember that cut, because it comes back later. They ate, they talked, and they went to sleep. And then, sometime that night, something scared them so badly that they didn't use the door. They cut through the wall and went out into the wind, barely dressed. Their footprints led down toward the forest, about a mile away. And here's the strange part: there were only their own footprints. Nobody else's. Under a tall cedar at the forest edge, they managed to light a small fire. Then three of them headed back uphill toward the tent. Why would you walk back to the place you just ran from? When nobody came home, students and soldiers went out to search. On the twenty-sixth of February, they found the tent, half buried in snow. It had been cut open from the inside. Boots, coats and food were all still there. Two of the hikers lay under the cedar, by the ashes of the fire. Three more lay on the slope between the tree and the tent. But four were still missing, and the snow kept them hidden for months. In May, the last four were found in a ravine near the cedar, under deep snow. And then the case turned dark. Some had serious chest and skull injuries, the kind you'd expect from a car crash. Yet the searchers found no signs of a fight. So who, or what, could do that? In May 1959, the Soviet investigators closed the case with one strange phrase. The nine had died because of a compelling natural force. For decades, people filled that gap with theories: secret military tests, strange lights in the sky, a deep sound called infrasound. An avalanche? Most people said no. The slope looked too gentle, and the searchers saw no clear avalanche. For sixty years, the mystery stayed open. Until a scientist in Switzerland got a phone call. In 2019, Russia reopened the case and blamed a snow slab, but never really explained how. That year, a journalist called Johan Gaume, an avalanche scientist at EPFL. He teamed up with Alexander Puzrin from ETH Zurich. Could the answer be in the snow itself? Remember that cut in the snow? Digging it removed the support under the snow above. All night, the wind blew more snow onto the slope, adding weight, slowly. At least nine hours later, a slab of snow broke loose. Not a huge avalanche. A block about the size of a car. Small enough to leave almost no trace, but heavy enough to crush part of the tent. And if you fear a bigger avalanche in the dark, running for the trees makes sense. Now here's the crazy part. To test the injuries, the team adapted the snow animation code from Disney's Frozen. They combined it with old car crash test data, and the injuries matched. Later expeditions found steeper slopes there, and in January 2022 they filmed two fresh snow slabs. Case closed? Not for everyone. Critics still doubt the snow and the wind, and many relatives were never convinced. Even the scientists say nobody knows exactly what happened. They only showed that a small avalanche could explain it. So what do you think drove them out of that tent: a slab of snow, or something we still don't understand?
