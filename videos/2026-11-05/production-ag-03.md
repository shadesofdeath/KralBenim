### Narration (one flowing text, 4508 chars, 50 scenes, ~5:32 by preview estimate)

A runner bursts into the stadium, way ahead of everyone. The crowd jumps to its feet. The President's daughter steps forward with a wreath, ready to crown the champion. There's just one problem. For about eleven miles of this race, he wasn't running. He was sitting in a car. And the man who really won? He was being kept on his feet with rat poison. This is the 1904 Olympic marathon, and every word of it is true. So how did the real winner end up nearly dying out there? To understand that, you have to see the course.

St. Louis, summer of 1904. The city is hosting a World's Fair, and the Olympics are part of it. On August 30th, at three in the afternoon, thirty-two men line up for the marathon. It's about 90 degrees. The course was nearly 25 miles of dirt roads. And here's the crazy part. In all those miles, there were only two places to drink: a water tower at mile six, and a well at mile twelve. That was on purpose. The chief organizer, James Sullivan, wanted to study what he called purposeful dehydration. Thirty-two runners were about to become his experiment!

The gun fired, and after five laps of the stadium, the runners hit the open road. That's when the cars showed up. Officials, doctors and coaches drove alongside the runners, and every car threw up a cloud of dust. You couldn't see. You couldn't breathe. Runners coughed and choked. One American, William Garcia, was found collapsed by the road. The dust had torn up his insides. Another hour, and he could have bled to death. And he wasn't even the strangest story of the day. Because one of the leaders had just found a much easier way to run a marathon!

His name was Fred Lorz. He led early, but around mile nine, cramps hit him and he quit. Then he climbed into a car. And as it rolled along the course, Lorz waved at the runners he was passing. Then, near mile nineteen, the car broke down. So Lorz did something unbelievable. He hopped out and started running again. He jogged into the stadium in first place, and the crowd went wild. Alice Roosevelt, the President's daughter, placed a wreath on his head. She was about to hand him the gold medal, when someone shouted that he hadn't run the race. It was just a joke, he said. Officials banned him for life. But while all this was happening, the real leader was still out on the road, and he was in terrible shape.

Thomas Hicks was leading by a mile and a half, but he was falling apart. With ten miles to go, he begged to lie down. His trainers said no. Instead, they gave him a dose of strychnine mixed with egg whites. Strychnine is rat poison. Back then, a tiny dose was thought to work as a stimulant. It's the first recorded use of drugs in the modern Olympics. Later, they gave him more, with brandy. By the end, he was hallucinating. He thought the finish was still twenty miles away. In the last stretch, his trainers held him up, and his feet shuffled across the line. His time: three hours, twenty-eight minutes, the slowest winning time in Olympic marathon history. It took four doctors to get him back on his feet. That was the winner. But wait until you hear about the man who came fourth.

Andarín Carvajal was a mail carrier from Cuba. On his way to St. Louis, he lost every cent gambling in New Orleans. So he hitchhiked the rest of the way. He ran in his street clothes, the trousers cut off at the knee. Starving, he stopped at an orchard and ate some apples. They were rotten. The cramps got so bad, he lay down by the road and took a nap. And he still finished fourth. Fourth! And he wasn't the only one. Len Taunyane and Jan Mashiani were Tswana men from South Africa. They were the first Black Africans ever to compete at the Olympics. Taunyane was running well, until wild dogs chased him about a mile off the course. He ran back, and still finished ninth. Mashiani came twelfth. Of thirty-two starters, only fourteen finished!

So what happened after all this? The race was such a scandal that the marathon was nearly dropped from the Olympics for good. Lorz's lifetime ban was cut to six months, and the next spring, he won the Boston Marathon. For real this time. And drugs? The Olympics didn't test athletes for them until 1968, sixty-four years later. So think back to that wreath on Fred Lorz's head. The crowd cheered a man who rode in a car, while the real winner could barely stand. Which of these runners do you think really deserved the gold? Tell me in the comments, and subscribe to Stickstory for more of history's strangest true stories.

### Narration after round 1 (4489 chars)

A runner bursts into the stadium, way ahead of everyone. The crowd jumps to its feet. The President's daughter steps forward with a wreath, ready to crown the champion. There's just one problem. For about eleven miles of this race, he wasn't running. He was sitting in a car. And the man who really won? He was being kept on his feet with rat poison. This is the 1904 Olympic marathon, and every word of it is true. So how did the real winner end up nearly dying out there? To understand that, you have to see the course. St. Louis, summer of 1904. The city is hosting a World's Fair, and the Olympics are part of it. On August 30th, at three in the afternoon, thirty-two men line up for the marathon. It's about 90 degrees. The course was nearly 25 miles of dirt roads. And here's the crazy part. In all those miles, there were only two places to drink: a water tower at mile six, and a well at mile twelve. That was on purpose. The chief organizer, James Sullivan, wanted to study what he called purposeful dehydration. Thirty-two runners were about to become his experiment! The gun fired, and after five laps of the stadium, the runners hit the open road. That's when the cars showed up. Officials, doctors and coaches drove alongside the runners, and every car threw up a cloud of dust. You couldn't see. You couldn't breathe. Runners coughed and choked. One American, William Garcia, was found collapsed by the road. The dust had torn up his insides. Another hour, and he could have bled to death. But he wasn't even the strangest story of the day. Because one of the leaders had just found a much easier way to run a marathon! His name was Fred Lorz. He led early, but around mile nine, cramps hit him and he quit. Then he climbed into a car. And as it rolled along the course, Lorz waved at the runners he was passing. Then, near mile nineteen, the car broke down. So Lorz did something unbelievable. He hopped out and started running again. He jogged into the stadium in first place, and the crowd went wild. Alice Roosevelt placed a wreath on his head. She was about to hand him the gold medal, when someone shouted that he hadn't run the race. It was just a joke, he said. Officials banned him for life. But while all this was happening, the real leader was still out on the road. And he was in terrible shape. Thomas Hicks was leading by a mile and a half, but he was falling apart. With ten miles to go, he begged to lie down. His trainers said no. Instead, they gave him a dose of strychnine mixed with egg whites. Strychnine is rat poison. Back then, a tiny dose was thought to work as a stimulant. It's the first recorded use of drugs in the modern Olympics. Later, they gave him more, with brandy. By the end, he was hallucinating. He thought the finish was still twenty miles away. In the last stretch, his trainers held him up, and his feet shuffled across the line. His time: three hours, twenty-eight minutes, the slowest winning time in Olympic marathon history. It took four doctors to get him back on his feet! That was the winner. But wait until you hear about the man who came fourth. Andarín Carvajal was a mail carrier from Cuba. On the way, he lost every cent gambling in New Orleans. So he hitchhiked the rest of the way. He ran in his street clothes, the trousers cut off at the knee. Starving, he stopped at an orchard and ate some apples. They were rotten. The cramps got so bad, he lay down by the road and took a nap. And he still finished fourth. Fourth! And he wasn't the only one. Len Taunyane and Jan Mashiani were Tswana men from South Africa. They were the first Black Africans ever to compete at the Olympics. Taunyane was running well, until wild dogs chased him about a mile off the course. He ran back and still finished ninth, and Mashiani came twelfth. Of thirty-two starters, only fourteen finished! But the story didn't end at the finish line. So what happened after all this? The race was such a scandal that the marathon was nearly dropped from the Olympics for good. Lorz's lifetime ban was cut to six months, and the next spring, he won the Boston Marathon. And drugs? The Olympics didn't test athletes for them until 1968, sixty-four years later. So think back to that wreath on Fred Lorz's head. The crowd cheered a man who rode in a car, while the real winner could barely stand. Which of these runners do you think deserved the gold?
