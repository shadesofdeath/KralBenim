**Narration** (4396 chars incl. spaces, 47 scenes, est. ~5:27):
> Picture this. You're hanging inside a crack in the ice, fourteen feet down, on a rope tied to a sledge. Above you, the sledge is creeping toward the edge. Below you, there's no bottom at all. The man on that rope is Douglas Mawson. His two friends are dead, his food is almost gone, and the soles of his feet have come away. So how did he climb out, and walk all the way home? Let me show you. To understand how he got there, we have to go back a year. In 1911, Mawson, a young geologist from Adelaide, took his expedition to Cape Denison, in Antarctica. Nobody had ever lived there. And without knowing it, he had picked one of the windiest places on Earth. Freezing air pours off the ice cap like water off a roof, and slams into the sea. Mawson wrote that gusts of one hundred and fifty miles per hour were common. One winter afternoon, the wind averaged ninety-seven for six hours straight. They called it the home of the blizzard. But Mawson didn't come to sit in a hut. He came to map a coast nobody had ever seen. On the tenth of November 1912, he set out east with two men and two dog sledges. Belgrave Ninnis was a young British army officer. Xavier Mertz was a Swiss ski champion. For five weeks, it worked. They crossed two huge glaciers and covered more than three hundred miles. Then, on the fourteenth of December, Mertz looked back over his shoulder. Where Ninnis and his sledge should have been, there was nothing. Just a hole in the snow. Far down, on a ledge, they saw one dog still alive. They called for hours. No answer ever came. And here's the worst part. That sledge carried the main tent, most of the food, and all of the dog food. They were more than three hundred miles from the hut, with about ten days of food. And nobody knew where they were. So they turned around, and did the only thing they could. Each time a dog collapsed, they killed it and ate it. Mawson wrote that the meat was tough and stringy. They ate the livers too. Remember that liver, because it comes back later. By New Year's Eve, Mertz was sick. His skin began to peel, and his strength just drained away. Mawson tied him onto the sledge and pulled him. On the eighth of January, around two in the morning, Mertz died in the tent. Mawson had no one left. Now he was alone, about one hundred miles from the hut. How do you even start that walk? First, he cut his sledge in half to make it lighter. Then he looked at his feet. The skin of each sole had come off in one piece. So he rubbed them with lanolin, bound them back on, and kept walking. And then, on the seventeenth of January, the snow gave way. He dropped into a crevasse and jolted to a stop, fourteen feet down on the rope. He pulled himself up, knot by knot, and reached the edge. Then he slipped, and slid all the way back down. In that dark moment, he remembered a line from a poem by Robert Service: just have one more try. So he did. One last huge effort, and he rolled out onto the snow. Then he made a rope ladder and tied it to the sledge, so no crevasse could trap him again. On the twenty-ninth of January, he saw something black flapping in the snow. It was a cairn left by a search party, with food and a note pointing the way home. The men had built it that same morning. He had missed them by hours. But soon, he would miss something much bigger. The last slope was steep ice, and the wind trapped him in an ice cave for a whole week. Finally, on the eighth of February 1913, he came over the last rise and saw the bay. Out on the water, the Aurora was sailing away. She had left only a few hours before. They called her back by radio, but the wind was so violent that no boat could reach the shore. Six men had stayed behind to search for him. So Mawson spent a second winter in the blizzard. Now, why did Mertz get so sick, so fast? One theory points straight at those dog livers. Husky liver can hold huge amounts of vitamin A, and too much of it makes your skin peel. Two researchers suggested this in 1969. Other doctors say starving dogs had lean livers, and hunger and cold were enough. Nobody knows for sure. Mawson sailed home in 1914. He was knighted that year, and later his face went on Australia's hundred dollar note. So what would have kept you walking: the poem, the men at the hut, or pure stubbornness? Tell me in the comments, and subscribe to Stickstory for more survival stories that sound impossible.
