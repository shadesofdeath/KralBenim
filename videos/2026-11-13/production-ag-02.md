People thought the stars were falling. On this night in 1833, up to 100,000 meteors an hour lit up America. Families knelt
to pray. But Professor Denison Olmsted asked where they came from. Reports poured in, and every streak pointed back to one
spot in Leo. So they came from space! Dust from a comet we fly through every November. The world didn't end. Meteor science
began. Subscribe for a true story like this every day.

Fact check (≥ 2 sources each):
- Night of 12–13 Nov 1833, eastern North America (Smithsonian, Linda Hall Library, Wikipedia/Leonids).
- Rate: Smithsonian gives 50,000–150,000 an hour, Linda Hall "some 100,000 meteors per hour" at the peak, and Space.com's
  Joe Rao (via Wikipedia) "over one hundred thousand an hour". The script says "up to 100,000", which sits inside the range.
- Praying / Judgment Day: Irish Times eyewitness ("some was screamin', and some was prayin'… they thought it was Judgement
  Day"); Explorersweb ("a harbinger of Christ's second coming to God's wrath").
- Olmsted, Yale professor, appealed through newspapers for observations, and the reports were forwarded to him (Smithsonian,
  Explorersweb). Radiant in Leo (Smithsonian, Linda Hall). The radiant moved with the stars, so the origin was cosmic
  (Linda Hall; Wikipedia: he said the meteors came from a cloud of particles in space).
- Comet 55P/Tempel–Tuttle is the source, identified in 1866–67. The Leonids return every mid-November, with storms about
  every 33 years (Wikipedia, Space.com, Explorersweb). The 2026 peak is 17 Nov (Space.com/IMO); it is not used in the
  narration because of the budget.
- "Meteor science began": Linda Hall calls it "the birth of the study of meteor showers".
